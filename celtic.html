Where the stories come from ▾

Almost everything we know about the Irish and Welsh gods was written down by medieval Christian monks, centuries after the old religion had faded. The monks often recast the gods as ancient kings, heroes and fairy-folk, so the texts show gods in disguise. The Gaulish and British gods are known mostly from Roman-era carvings and inscriptions, with few stories attached. Treat any single detail as one voice among several.

Overlaps and "see also" ▾

The Celtic traditions share roots, so the same god often appears in several places under different names. Lugh in Ireland, Lleu in Wales and Lugus in Gaul are probably one figure. Nuada, Nudd and Nodens may be another. Where entries are linked this way, a short "See also" note says so. They are scholarly guesses and not settled.

A note on festivals ▾

The Irish tradition names four quarter days: Imbolc (February 1), Beltane (May 1), Lughnasadh (August 1) and Samhain (November 1). They appear in medieval Irish texts, though what people did on them is mostly known from later folk custom. Modern pagans group them into the Wheel of the Year. Where no festival is attested, the entry says so.
